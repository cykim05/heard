## 패치 2 — §3.6 (신규) BM25 sparse baseline

**삽입 위치:** §3.5 (line 815–862) **뒤**, §4 Discussion 앞에 신설.

### 3.6 Sparse vs dense retrieval (Table 9; Figure 3)

The v0.2 sweep used a single dense `multilingual-e5-small` cosine retriever
and noted (§4.7) the absence of a sparse baseline to isolate its contribution.
We add one here: BM25 Okapi (`rank_bm25`) with kiwipiepy morpheme tokenization,
the same tokenizer applied to corpus and query. Only the retriever changes —
Algorithm 2 prompt assembly, the advisory policy, top-k = 5, the per-persona
`ko_native` indices, and the contains-token criterion are reused unchanged, and
generation stays at `max_tokens = 200`, `temperature = 0.3`, `seed = 42`. We
report the two anchor SUTs `kanana_nano` and `kanana_8b` on the 64 non-REFL
`ko_native` items. As an integrity check, re-running dense retrieval through the
same harness reproduces the v0.2 figures exactly (`kanana_nano` 10.9 %,
`kanana_8b` 18.8 %) with the dense top-5 document ids matching the v0.2 sweep on
64/64 items, confirming the wiring before any sparse comparison.

[Table 9 here — SUT × {no_node, BM25, dense, Δ(dense−BM25), gold-hit@5 dense,
gold-hit@5 BM25}, each pass rate with its Wilson 95 % CI;
source `_tabledata/table9_bm25.csv`]

[Figure 3 here — `figures/v0.3/figure3.pdf`]

Two observations separate retrieval quality from reader behavior. First, the
two retrievers are equivalent at *finding* the evidence: gold-hit@5 — the share
of the 54 evidence-bearing items whose gold utterance id lands in the top-5 — is
identical at 38.9 % (21/54) for dense and BM25. Second, they are not equivalent
*downstream*, and the direction flips with scale. On `kanana_8b` dense beats
BM25 by +7.8 pp (18.8 % vs 10.9 %), but on `kanana_nano` BM25 is *ahead* of
dense by 3.1 pp (14.1 % vs 10.9 %); both retrievers clear the memoryless
baseline (4.7 % / 1.6 %) comfortably. Because the two retrievers surface gold
evidence at the same rate, the contains-pass gap cannot be a recall effect — it
is the reader model's use of an equally-good context. Dense retrieval's value on
this benchmark is therefore better described as "a larger model uses it better"
than as "it retrieves better"; at the 2.1 B on-device scale that Part 1 targets,
a far cheaper sparse retriever is not measurably worse.

## 패치 3 — §3.7 (신규) Answer-first guardrail falsification

**삽입 위치:** §3.6 **뒤**(여전히 §4 앞).

### 3.7 The answer-first guardrail does not close the regression (Table 10; Figure 4)

§4.2 raised a falsifiable prediction: that an "answer-first" guardrail —
*"Answer the question first. Do not re-narrate the past."* — would close most of
the retrieval > oracle gap on the affected 7–8 B SUTs by suppressing context
recapitulation. We test it directly. The guardrail is added as a single line
prepended to the advisory system prompt (policy `advisory_answerfirst`); nothing
else changes, so the guardrail's effect is isolated from any other policy
difference. We re-run only the `oracle` column under this policy on the four
reversal SUTs (`kanana_8b`, `kanana_8b_int4`, `qwen25_7b`, `bllossom_8b`),
64 non-REFL `ko_native` items, same seed and decoding settings; the
`oracle_base` and `retrieval` columns are the existing v0.2 values.

[Table 10 here — SUT × {oracle_base, oracle_af, retrieval, gap-closed, Δlength,
4-gram overlap base→af}; source `_tabledata/table10_answerfirst.csv`]

[Figure 4 here — `figures/v0.3/figure4.pdf`]

The prediction is not confirmed. Only two of the four SUTs improve at all
(`kanana_8b` +3.1 pp, `qwen25_7b` +1.6 pp), and neither reaches its own
retrieval level — they close 28.6 % and 33.3 % of the gap respectively. The
other two move the wrong way (`kanana_8b_int4` −4.7 pp, `bllossom_8b` −1.6 pp),
and the mean fraction of the gap closed across the four is −5.8 %. The mechanism
the prediction assumed is also absent: while response length does fall for the
Kanana pair (216 → 181 and 208 → 179 characters), the character-4-gram overlap
between the response and the oracle evidence — our recapitulation proxy — is
essentially unchanged (e.g. 0.015 → 0.017 on `kanana_8b`) and even rises on
`kanana_8b_int4` (0.016 → 0.025). Recapitulation overlap is already low (≈ 0.02)
in the baseline, so the regression was never driven by verbatim re-narration the
guardrail could remove. We therefore record the v0.2 §4.2 prediction as
**refuted in its strong form** (it does not close the regression) and at best
weakly partial for `kanana_8b` alone; the regression's cause is not a
prompt-surface re-narration habit and requires a different account (§4.2,
revised).

## 패치 4 — §4.2 개정 (기존 문단 교체)

**교체 위치:** §4.2 "The retrieval > oracle regression" 전체(line 875–892)를
아래로 **교체**. (예측을 세우는 마지막 문장이 이제는 검증 결과를 가리킨다.)

### 4.2 The retrieval > oracle regression

Five of eleven v0.2 configurations record `oracle` strictly below `retrieval`
on `ko_native` (Table 1), with the regression concentrated in the 7–8 B
parameter tier (`kanana_8b`, `kanana_8b_int4`, `qwen25_7b`, `bllossom_8b`) and
absent in the two smallest SUTs (`kanana_nano`, `hclova_seed_15b`). We initially
read this as a long-context summarization bias: a 15-document oracle prompt
roughly triples the length of a 5-neighbor retrieval prompt, and the larger SUTs
might respond by recapitulating the evidence sessions rather than answering the
today-question. That account yields a falsifiable prediction — an "answer-first"
guardrail (*"Answer the question first. Do not re-narrate the past."*) should
close most of the gap — which we test in v0.3 (§3.7).

The prediction fails. Adding the guardrail as a minimal one-line variant of the
advisory prompt and re-running the oracle column on the four affected SUTs
closes, on average, −5.8 % of the retrieval-vs-oracle gap: only two SUTs improve
at all, none reaches retrieval level, and two regress further (Table 10,
Figure 4). The assumed mechanism is also unsupported — character-4-gram overlap
between response and oracle evidence is unchanged even where response length
drops — so the regression is not a verbatim re-narration habit that a
prompt-surface instruction can suppress. We now read the regression as a genuine
context-utilization effect rather than a prompt-design artifact: the larger SUTs
extract the answer *less* reliably from a longer, cleaner gold context than from
a shorter top-5 retrieval context, even though the gold context strictly
contains the evidence. The §3.6 finding that retrieval quality (gold-hit@5) is
not the bottleneck points the same way — the limiting factor is how the reader
uses available context, not what is placed in front of it. Characterizing this
utilization gap (e.g., position-in-prompt sensitivity, evidence-count scaling)
is the open question the failed guardrail leaves behind.

## 패치 5 — §4.8 (신규) Judge reliability and position bias

**삽입 위치:** §4.7 (line 968–988) **뒤**, §5 Conclusion 앞에 신설.

### 4.8 Judge reliability and position bias (Table 8)

The pairwise reflective-quality results in §3.4 rest on an LLM-as-judge, which
is itself subject to the position bias documented for MT-Bench-style judging
(Zheng et al., 2023). We audit the existing 2,112 v0.2 verdicts without any new
judge calls. Because each (item, SUT, condition, rubric, judge) pair was scored
in both A/B presentation orders, we can measure *order-consistency* — whether the
two orders agree on the canonical winner after inverting the swap — and
*position preference* — the share of physical first-slot picks among non-tie
decisions (0.5 = unbiased). Inter-judge agreement between `gpt-4o-mini` and
`claude-haiku-4.5` is reported per rubric as raw agreement and Cohen's κ. We do
not report repetition stability: identical judge calls hit the diskcache, so a
re-run is a tautology.

[Table 8 here — rubric × {order-consistency, Cohen κ, slot-A rate} + Overall;
source `_tabledata/table8_judge_reliability.csv`]

The reliability profile maps cleanly onto the §3.4 result. The two rubrics that
carry the "mirror, do not advise" thesis — emotional attunement and open-question
framing — are the most reliable: order-consistency 0.83 and 0.82, inter-judge κ
0.42 and 0.49 (moderate). The two weaker rubrics are also the less reliable
ones: specificity at consistency 0.60 / κ 0.21, and non-directive collapsing to
consistency 0.34 / κ 0.02 (chance-level inter-judge agreement). The non-directive
collapse is concentrated in a single judge: `gpt-4o-mini` is order-consistent on
that rubric only 1.5 % of the time and picks the second slot 83 % of the time,
i.e. it answers almost purely by position regardless of content, whereas
`claude-haiku-4.5` is consistent 67 % of the time on the same rubric. Overall the
judges lean slightly toward the second-presented response (slot-A share 0.385),
so position bias is present but modest in aggregate and is symmetrized away by
the A/B-swap design for the headline rubrics. The upshot for §3.4 is reassuring:
the 88 % reflective-share figures stand on the two position-stable, moderately
agreeing rubrics, while the weaker specificity/non-directive margins should be
read with the position artifact in mind and not over-interpreted.
